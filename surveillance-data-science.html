Surveillance Data Science

This is the name I use for a line of research that has gradually emerged from my work on infectious disease transmission in wildlife, livestock, and human populations.

The starting point is simple: surveillance data are observations of an infection process, not the process itself. What we see depends on where we look, how intensively we look, who gets tested, and what can be detected and reported.

What do the data actually tell us, and what should we observe next?

What I am trying to understand

My earlier work was mainly about transmission: how infections spread through space, between individuals, and across host populations. Working with surveillance data made another problem increasingly difficult to ignore. The observed pattern may reflect both the underlying infection process and the way surveillance was carried out.

I therefore treat the observation process as part of the epidemiological problem. From there, the question naturally moves from estimating current risk to deciding whether another source of information is worth collecting, and eventually to deciding where limited surveillance effort should go next.

Questions I am working on

  • How much can we infer about latent infection risk from incomplete and selectively collected surveillance data?
  • When does information from another host or surveillance source add something genuinely new?
  • When does better prediction actually change a surveillance or control decision?
  • Where should the next sample, survey, or surveillance effort be placed?
  • When is an existing surveillance network good enough, and when should it be adjusted?

What counts as useful information?

I find it useful to separate three questions that are often treated as the same thing.

Information value Does a new source reduce uncertainty or provide a signal that is not already contained in the existing data?
Predictive value Does that information improve estimation or help identify high-risk places that would otherwise be missed?
Decision value Does the improvement change which locations, hosts, or samples should actually be prioritised?

Current directions

African swine fever
Wild boar and domestic pig surveillance — when does information from one host improve inference or decisions for the other?
Human respiratory infections
Clinical and wastewater surveillance — what is gained when the same epidemic is observed through a different observation process?
Highly pathogenic avian influenza
Wild birds, poultry, and mammals — which host information is complementary, redundant, or most useful to collect first?
One Health systems
Human, animal, vector, and environmental data — how should heterogeneous surveillance streams be combined when resources are limited?

These are test beds for the same set of surveillance questions, rather than separate research programmes.

How I approach the problem

I use Bayesian hierarchical and spatiotemporal models to separate latent infection from observation, mechanistic models when transmission structure matters, and simulation-based virtual surveillance experiments when competing surveillance strategies cannot be compared directly in the real world. For the design problem, I am interested in value-of-information analysis, Bayesian experimental design, and active learning.

Longer term

The longer-term aim is not to build a single optimal surveillance network. I am more interested in the conditions under which a current design remains adequate, when targeted additional information is worth collecting, and when the surveillance system itself needs to change.

In that sense, Surveillance Data Science is the part of my research that connects inference to the next decision: from imperfect observations, to an estimate of what is happening, to a choice about what to observe next.
