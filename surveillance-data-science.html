Surveillance Data Science

From imperfect observations to better surveillance decisions.

Surveillance Data Science asks what surveillance data reveal about latent infection risk, when additional information improves inference and decisions, and how the next observation should be designed under limited resources.

Research framework

Infer

What is happening beyond what we observe?

Model latent infection processes separately from observation processes such as detection, surveillance effort, sampling, testing, and reporting.

Evaluate

When does additional information have value?

Evaluate whether another host or data source adds new information, improves prediction, and changes surveillance or control decisions.

Design

What should we observe next?

Compare candidate observations across locations, hosts, and samples to identify where additional surveillance can most reduce uncertainty and improve decisions.

Adapt

When should a surveillance system change?

Develop robust and adaptive strategies that can be maintained, adjusted, or redesigned as risks, information, and surveillance objectives change.

Quantitative approaches

  • Bayesian hierarchical models separating latent infection and observation processes
  • Simulation-based virtual surveillance experiments
  • Information value, predictive value, and decision value
  • Bayesian experimental design and active learning
  • Spatial and spatiotemporal modelling of infectious disease risk

Example systems

African swine fever

Wild boar and domestic pig surveillance as a two-host system for studying the conditional value of additional host information.

Highly pathogenic avian influenza

Wild birds, poultry, and mammals as a more complex multi-host system in which information can be redundant, complementary, or differently prioritized.

Human respiratory infections

Clinical and wastewater surveillance as parallel observation processes of the same underlying community infection dynamics.

Zoonotic and vector-borne systems

Integration of human, animal, vector, and environmental observations to evaluate when multiple data sources improve One Health surveillance.
