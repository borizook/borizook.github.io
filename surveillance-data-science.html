Surveillance Data Science

My research asks how much we can learn from imperfect surveillance data, when an additional source of information is genuinely useful, and how those answers should change what we observe next.

Central question

What do the data actually tell us, and what should we observe next?

Research framework

Surveillance data are generated by both an infection process and an observation process. My work treats these two processes separately, then connects inference to the design of the next observation.

01
Observation
Detection, sampling effort, accessibility, testing, and reporting determine how infection becomes visible in surveillance data.
02
Inference
Separate the observation process from the latent infection process and estimate the underlying spatial or temporal risk.
03
Added information
Ask whether another host, data source, or surveillance stream adds information that is not already contained in the existing system.
04
Surveillance design
Use the remaining uncertainty and decision problem to choose what, where, or when to observe next under limited resources.
05
Adaptation
Evaluate when the existing design remains adequate and when new risks or information justify adjustment or redesign.

Questions I am working on

  1. How much can we infer about latent infection risk from incomplete and selectively collected surveillance data?
  2. When does information from another host or surveillance source provide a genuinely new signal?
  3. When does improved prediction change a surveillance or control decision?
  4. Where should the next sample, survey, or surveillance effort be placed?
  5. When should an existing surveillance network be maintained, adjusted, or redesigned?

Evaluating additional information

I separate the value of a new data source into three questions. Better prediction is useful, but it matters most when it changes what we would actually do.

Information value Does it reduce uncertainty or reveal a signal that was previously missing?
→
Predictive value Does it improve risk estimation or identify high-risk places that would otherwise be missed?
→
Decision value Does the improvement change which locations, hosts, or samples should be prioritised?

Systems I use to test these questions

These disease systems are not separate research programmes. I use them to test the same surveillance questions under different host structures and observation processes.

African swine fever
Wild boar and domestic pig surveillance; testing when information from one host improves inference or decisions for the other.
Human respiratory infections
Clinical and wastewater surveillance; comparing different observation processes for the same underlying epidemic.
Highly pathogenic avian influenza
Wild birds, poultry, and mammals; evaluating which host information is complementary, redundant, or most useful to collect first.
One Health systems
Human, animal, vector, and environmental data; studying how heterogeneous surveillance streams should be combined when resources are limited.

Methods

Depending on the question, I use Bayesian hierarchical and spatiotemporal models to distinguish latent infection from observation, mechanistic models when transmission structure matters, and simulation-based virtual surveillance experiments when competing surveillance strategies cannot be compared directly in the real world. For surveillance design, I am interested in value-of-information analysis, Bayesian experimental design, and active learning.

Longer-term direction

The aim is not to build one universally optimal surveillance network. I want to understand the conditions under which a current design is sufficient, when targeted additional information is worth collecting, and when the surveillance system itself should change.

In this sense, Surveillance Data Science connects inference to the next decision: from imperfect observations, to an estimate of what is happening, to a choice about what should be observed next.
