Research programme

Surveillance Data Science

From imperfect observations to better surveillance decisions.

Core question

What should we observe next, and where should limited surveillance resources be allocated?

Why surveillance data need a science of their own

Surveillance data do not directly reveal the underlying infection process. Detection probability, surveillance effort, accessibility, sampling, testing, reporting, and differences between data sources shape what becomes visible. My research separates these observation processes from latent infection risk, then asks whether new information is valuable enough to change inference and real surveillance decisions.

Research principle

More data are not automatically better data. The important question is whether an additional host, data source, location, or sample provides information that changes what we infer or what we decide to do.

From observation to action

A sequential research framework links latent infection processes, observation processes, the value of additional information, and the design of the next observation.

01

Infer

What is happening beyond what we observe?

Separate latent infection processes from detection, surveillance effort, accessibility, testing, sampling, and reporting.

02

Evaluate

When does additional information have value?

Test whether another host or data source contributes new information rather than repeating what is already known.

03

Design

What should we observe next?

Compare locations, hosts, and samples to identify the next observation that best reduces uncertainty and improves decisions.

04

Adapt

When should the surveillance system change?

Develop robust and adaptive designs that can be maintained, adjusted, or redesigned as risks and information change.

What counts as valuable information?

I distinguish statistical improvement from practical value by evaluating additional surveillance information at three levels.

01

Information value

Does it add information that was not already available?

Reduction in uncertainty about latent risk and conditional information beyond existing surveillance.

02

Predictive value

Does it improve risk estimation or detection of high-risk areas?

Improvement in estimation error, sensitivity, specificity, or identification of epidemiologically important areas.

03

Decision value

Does better prediction actually change surveillance or control choices?

Changes in priority areas, missed-risk reduction, or avoidance of unnecessary surveillance and response.

Quantitative approaches

The methods are selected to answer the surveillance question, not treated as ends in themselves.

Bayesian hierarchical modelling Latent-process models Spatial & spatiotemporal modelling Simulation-based virtual surveillance Value of information Bayesian experimental design Active learning AI & machine-learning prediction

Systems for testing general principles

Different disease systems provide different combinations of host structure and observation processes, allowing the same surveillance questions to be tested under increasingly complex conditions.

African swine fever
Wild boar · Domestic pigs

A two-host system for testing when wildlife surveillance adds information beyond livestock surveillance, and under which epidemiological and observation conditions that information changes decisions.

Highly pathogenic avian influenza
Wild birds · Poultry · Mammals

A more complex multi-host system for studying redundancy, complementarity, and the order in which host-specific surveillance information should be added.

Human respiratory infections
Clinical surveillance · Wastewater surveillance

A multi-source surveillance setting in which different observation processes capture the same underlying community infection dynamics.

One Health systems
Human · Animal · Vector · Environment

A longer-term setting for linking heterogeneous data sources to surveillance resource allocation and testing how general surveillance-design principles perform in real systems.

Long-term direction

Surveillance should be treated as a sequential decision system. The goal is to infer what is currently hidden, identify what information is still missing, design the next observation, and update the surveillance system as new risks and evidence emerge.
